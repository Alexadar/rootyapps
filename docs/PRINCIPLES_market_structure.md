# Principles — how niche software markets are structured

> Crystallised from a research programme spanning ~30 sweeps of the App Store. Every rule below was
> learned by getting it wrong first. Deliberately general: no product names, because the mechanisms
> outlive the instances.
>
> The *method* for finding candidates lives in `marketing/candidate.finder.md`. This file is *why the
> method works*.

---

## 1. The single constraint

**Impressions, not quality.** A well-built app in a real niche can take single-digit impressions per
week and sell nothing. The funnel *below* the impression converts at ordinary rates; there is simply
nothing entering it.

Consequence: **never evaluate a candidate on math quality, oracle depth, or feature completeness.**
None of those has ever been the binding constraint. Evaluate on whether anyone will ever see it.

---

## 2. Who gives the calculation away, and why

### The output-type rule — the strongest predictor found

> **A vendor gives a calculation away free when its output is a purchase quantity.
> Nobody captures a calculation whose output is a machine setting.**

Quantity-output is *the order form*. Whoever sells the material will always publish the calculator,
always price it at zero, and always outspend an independent developer. Paint, fabric, fertiliser,
irrigation runtime, material takeoff, weight-per-length — all permanently closed.

Setting-output — an angle, a mark, a pinning chart, a bend deduction — has no attached sale. Nobody
profits from giving it away, so nobody does.

**Use as a pre-filter before measuring anything: if the answer is "how much to buy," it is dead.**

### The free giant is usually not a software company

The incumbent that closes a niche is typically an **equipment manufacturer, a distributor, or an
industry platform**, giving the tool away as marketing for a physical product. Their app is a cost
centre; yours would be a business. You cannot win that.

**Always check whether "Free" is actually free.** A listing can show no price while the description
reveals a time-limited trial — that is a *paid product with a funnel*, which is payment proof, not a
giveaway. Read the description before classifying.

---

## 3. Frequency beats audience size

**Apps that own a trade succeed. Apps that do a calculation fail.**

A job performed daily by the same person sustains an app; one performed annually never will, however
large the notional audience or well-built the tool. The same math, the same licensing and the same
quality produce opposite outcomes purely on how often the person needs it.

This also explains why enormous web-calculator demand does not translate: an occasional need is
Googled once and abandoned, never installed.

---

## 4. Point of work

**Does the practitioner compute this standing up holding the workpiece, or sitting at a desk inside
software their employer already bought?**

Desk-bound trades are invisible to a phone app *even at daily frequency*, because the calculation
already happens on a bigger screen inside a system of record. They pass every other gate and still
return no search demand.

**Empty search volume in a high-frequency trade usually means "they already do this elsewhere," not
"they don't need it."** That is unrecoverable by building something better.

---

## 5. Surface area defends price

A niche that reduces to **one formula** attracts a shovelware farm: many sellers, rapid entry, and a
best-in-field paid app with single-digit ratings. Nobody can charge for something copyable in an
afternoon.

A niche with **many interacting cases** sustains real prices, because breadth is expensive to
replicate and visible to the buyer.

---

## 6. Web demand is not store demand

A large majority of specialist calculation topics with substantial web traffic return **zero** app-store
search volume. Web reference sites host thousands of calculators because that demand is served by a
page visited once.

**That is an SEO channel, not an ASO channel.** They are different distribution systems, and a paid
offline app cannot enter the first one. Never infer store demand from web popularity.

---

## 7. Payment proof — the corrected rule

A hard "nobody is earning here, so skip it" gate is **wrong**. It excludes every unexploited market by
definition, because *unexploited* means nobody is earning. Applied strictly, it only ever approves
niches where a competitor already succeeds.

What payment evidence actually tests is **does this audience buy at all** — and that is only decisive
when they have demonstrably refused.

| Situation | Read |
|---|---|
| No paid incumbent **+ a thriving free one** | The audience was offered paid and chose free. **Dead.** |
| No paid incumbent **+ nobody has tried properly** | Open market. **This is the opportunity.** |
| A healthy paid incumbent exists | De-risked, but you are entering a fight |

**The decisive question is "is there a free giant," not "is anyone making money."**

---

## 8. Switching cost decides whether a wound is reachable

A loud grievance is worthless if the customer cannot leave. Where the incumbent holds the user's
library, history or documents, anger does not produce exit — people stay furious and stay put, and
displacing them means building a migration tool with an app attached.

**Heal where leaving is free.** Self-contained jobs — where the user brings nothing and takes nothing —
convert grievance into switching. Library-shaped products do not.

**And distinguish the wound.** A beloved product that betrayed its users leaves a salvageable audience
of proven buyers. A predatory product that defrauded people leaves nothing — those users were tricked,
not underserved, and there is no product they were waiting for.

---

## 9. Method: invert the search

Enumerating keywords and testing each is slow and mostly measures supply. Instead:

> **Census the catalogue for paid apps above a meaningful rating floor, then work backwards to the job.**

Payable niches cannot hide — money shows up as paid rating mass whether or not you guessed the term.
This is cheap and, crucially, **terminal**: when it returns the same names it returned last time, the
space is exhausted and further sweeping is waste.

Use **combined paid ratings in a field** as the single discriminator. It separates real from imaginary
cleanly, and it does not care how good your idea is.

---

## 10. Two failure modes in reading the evidence

**Rich autocomplete can be supply, not demand.** If the suggested completions are all existing product
names, the term is saturated rather than wanted. A *generic* completion is demand; a completion that is
somebody's product is supply.

**Rating averages can be manufactured.** A large gap between a lifetime average and recent sentiment is
the signature of prompted ratings harvested at moments of delight, against organic reviews written
later by irritated people. In such categories only the **content of complaints** is trustworthy —
nobody buys a review saying the ads ruined it.

# Principles — what people pay for, and what they punish

> Crystallised from reading several thousand App Store reviews across utilities, professional tools,
> creative software and games. General by design: no product names, because the patterns repeat and
> the instances don't matter.

---

## 1. The universal wound is betrayal, not price

Across every category examined, the dominant complaint was never "this costs too much" or "this lacks
a feature." It was **"I paid, and you charged me again."**

The recurring shapes, in the customers' own framing:

- Bought a one-time app, later discovered core tools locked behind a monthly fee
- Bought the "no ads" version and still got ads
- Held a lifetime unlock that stopped being honoured
- Features they already owned moved behind a paywall in an update
- A listing showing a low price that turns out to be a gate to an annual subscription

**These people are proven buyers who were cheated.** That is the most valuable audience in software:
pre-qualified, actively angry, and looking. It is categorically different from an audience that has
never paid for anything.

## 2. What they reward, unprompted

The mirror image appears in the highest-rated products, stated without being asked:

- *"No subscription, single payment"*
- *"One time payment and not another subscription"*
- *"No tracking and no subscription"*

Buyers volunteer this in five-star reviews. In several categories the phrase **"no ads" appears in the
product name itself** and outsells competitors on that basis alone.

**Being trustworthy about money is a feature people search for.**

---

## 3. But buy-once is only a *weapon* where competitors abuse subscriptions

This is the distinction that matters strategically.

- In categories where incumbents bolt subscriptions onto purchases, **refusing to do so is a
  differentiator buyers actively seek**.
- In categories where incumbents are honest little paid apps that merely stopped being updated,
  **nobody mentions pricing model at all** — there, buy-once is not a differentiator, it is just a price.

Check which world a candidate lives in before making pricing the pitch.

---

## 4. On-device compute is what makes buy-once structurally possible

**A product that calls an API has a recurring per-user cost, and recurring cost forces recurring
revenue.** That is why cloud-backed tools must subscribe — not greed, arithmetic.

Move the same work onto the device and marginal cost is zero. Buy-once stops being a concession and
becomes **structurally cheaper for you than for a cloud competitor**, while also making two other
claims literally true: works offline, and nothing leaves the device.

**The honest counterweight:** for heavy models, on-device is *slower* than a server GPU — sometimes by
an order of magnitude. You are trading the user's minutes for their privacy and their wallet. That
trade is only obviously good when the model is small enough to also be *faster* (no upload, no queue),
or when the work genuinely cannot be uploaded.

---

## 5. Discovery: the free funnel is how paid products are found

Where a developer ships both a free and a paid version of the same product, the free one consistently
carries **an order of magnitude or more** of the ratings, and converts a modest single-digit percentage
to paid.

The implication is uncomfortable for a pure paid-upfront strategy: **the paid tier is largely invisible
on its own.** It is reached *through* the free one. A product with no funnel forgoes the only discovery
mechanism the category demonstrably uses.

Two honest riders:
- In several observed pairs the paid version was **worse** than its free twin — fewer features, staler
  data — with the entire difference being the absence of advertising. Buyers noticed and punished it.
- A free tier that is a crippled demo produces the "this is not free" backlash. It must be genuinely
  complete enough to keep.

---

## 6. Price is anchored against the alternative, not against other apps

The highest prices sustained anywhere were in tools that replace **equipment**. Buyers compare against
a thousand-dollar instrument, not against a free app, and say so explicitly. That anchoring supports
prices far above normal app pricing.

Where the alternative is another app, price collapses toward the category floor.

**Find the expensive thing you replace, and let the buyer make that comparison.**

---

## 7. Perceived value is counted in units, not hours

Complaints about length almost never cite duration. They cite **countable chunks** — "only five
levels," "for the price."

Two products of the same total size, one divided into twice as many discrete units, draw dramatically
different complaint rates — the more granular one is perceived as substantial, the coarser one as thin.

**Ship enough discrete units that the number itself reads as generous, and price beneath what that
number implies.** At low prices the value question never arises; at higher ones you must be visibly
substantial.

---

## 8. If IAP is used, describe it exactly

The measured anger is never at in-app purchase as a mechanism. It is at **deception**. The rules that
follow directly from the complaints:

1. The free tier must be **genuinely usable**, not a demo
2. State the purchase, its price, and its **one-time** nature in the opening lines of the description
3. Say explicitly **what is not gated** — naming the boundary is what converts suspicion into trust
4. Never let a listed price imply the total cost when it doesn't

---

## 9. The shape of a healable target

Combining the above, a grievance is worth acting on only when all four hold:

| Condition | Why |
|---|---|
| **Proven paid audience** | demonstrated willingness, not just irritation |
| **Low data lock-in** | otherwise anger never becomes exit |
| **Self-contained job** | nothing to migrate |
| **The incumbent can't simply reverse it** | a large company can un-paywall overnight and erase your only advantage |

Loud wounds attached to hostage libraries are somebody else's business. Quiet wounds with zero
switching cost are reachable.
